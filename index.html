🚦 TrafficMind: Navi Mumbai signal timing

Can PSO beat fixed-time signals in Navi Mumbai?

This runs a vehicle-by-vehicle simulation (IDM car-following, random arrivals from a fixed seed) and a Particle Swarm Optimizer that scores each timing plan by running that simulation. The junction names are just labels and the traffic volumes are invented, not surveyed.

Conventional fixed-time

Green times are set in advance (30 s + 30 s by default, 3 s amber, 2 s all-red) and never change during a run. Simple and predictable, but it can't tell which approach has the longer queue.

PSO-optimized timing

Each particle is a candidate plan x=[g₁,g₂]. The swarm looks for green splits that keep delay and queues low in the simulation. An optional rolling-horizon mode re-optimizes at cycle boundaries using the arrivals it has seen so far.

Why fixed-time plans struggle

When demand shifts (say, heavy north–south flow), an equal split wastes green on the quiet approach while the busy one builds a queue it can't clear in one green. PSO can rebalance the split, but it won't always win. A plan tuned for one demand pattern can do worse when demand changes mid-run, and the "Sudden surge" scenario can show that.

PSO in simple words

PSO is a search method loosely modeled on a flock of birds. Each particle is one possible timing plan. Over many rounds the particles drift toward plans that cut delay, pulled by their own best find and the swarm's best find. It can't promise the best possible answer.

How PSO works here

  1. Generate particles. Random green plans [g₁,g₂].
  2. Simulate each plan and compute fitness J = α·D/D_ref + β·Q/Q_ref (D: mean delay, Q: time-averaged queue, both divided by the fixed-time baseline; α=1, β=0.3). Vehicles still waiting at the end count their delay too.
  3. Personal best pᵢ for each particle.
  4. Global best g for the swarm.
  5. Update: vᵢ←w·vᵢ+c₁r₁(pᵢ−xᵢ)+c₂r₂(g−xᵢ), xᵢ←xᵢ+vᵢ (w 0.9→0.4, c₁=c₂=1.5).
  6. Repair to 7–60 s greens within a 110 s total green budget, rounded to whole seconds.
  7. Repeat, then switch to the best valid plan at a cycle boundary. No guarantee it's the global optimum.

Research background

Paper 1: Abdul Kareem & Mejbel, Traffic Light Controller Module Based on PSO, American Journal of Artificial Intelligence 2018, 2(1):7–15

  • PSO picks which set of adjacent streets gets green. Fitness is a linear priority score |PC+PE+PSW−PP| (crowded, emergency vehicle, roadwork, pedestrian), fed by a separate camera-based monitor module.
  • It reports "flow ratio" gains of roughly 85–96% over several multi-light layouts, with PSO needing about 80–90 iterations. The paper notes PSO has to finish within the cycle time to be useful.
  • Limitations (our reading): no vehicle-level traffic simulation, so its percentages can't be compared with the delay numbers here.

Paper 2: Diaf, Tolba & Nait Sidi Moh, Traffic Urban Control Using an Intelligent PSO Algorithm Based on Integrated Approach, Algerian Journal of Signals and Systems 2020, 5(1)

  • Each particle is a signal cycle program whose dimensions are phase durations. Fitness comes from a macroscopic LWR / continuous Petri-net model with Rakha vehicle dynamics and Poisson arrivals.
  • Reported tuning: swarm sizes 20–200, c₁=c₂=2.05, convergence around 200+ iterations, and cycle times of about 71–74 s for a single intersection.
  • Limitations: macroscopic model, one tested intersection, and no fixed-time baseline comparison in the parts we read.

What we took from the papers, and what we changed

  • From the literature: a particle is a timing plan, phase durations are the variables, the standard velocity/position update, and Poisson arrivals.
  • Our own choices: a microscopic IDM simulator, a delay + queue objective scaled by a baseline, held-out test seeds, a repair step, the rolling-horizon extension, and a fixed-time baseline.

How this differs from real traffic control

One lane per approach, straight movements only, no pedestrians, no sensor noise, perfect driver compliance, and two phases. The rolling-horizon mode starts each internal simulation from an empty network, using arrival rates estimated from past counts, and applies the result one cycle later. Noisy estimates and compute time limit it. Future work: turning movements, pedestrian phases, emergency priority, sensor uncertainty, and real data. Educational simulation only, not for deployment.
